# Functional-parts catalogue

Use this when you are about to build a class of asset you haven't interrogated this way before.
It is a prompt for your own thinking, not a checklist to satisfy — the point is always the same
question: **what parts exist because this thing works?** If you model the parts that answer that,
the asset stops being a primitive with a texture on it.

The seven prompts, restated: **joined · supported · moves/opens · gripped/carried · fixed down ·
weathered · maintained.**

---

## Buildings and architecture

**Walls.** Walls are made of something in courses or panels: brick coursing with a header pattern,
timber framing with visible posts and braces, plaster over a lath ground with a base plinth where
it meets the wet. A wall meets the ground at a plinth or damp course, not at a clean intersection
with a ground plane — the wall is thicker or plinthed at the bottom because the bottom gets wet
and kicked.

**Openings.** Every opening has a **lintel** (or arch) carrying the load above it, **jambs** at
the sides, a **sill** shedding water at the bottom, and a **reveal** — the door or window sits
*back* in the thickness of the wall. A door coplanar with its wall is the single most common tell
that a building was extruded rather than built. Add: hinges on the side they actually swing,
handle/pull, threshold, and a frame that stands proud of the plaster.

**Roofs.** Roofs have an **eave that overhangs** far enough to keep rain off the wall, **rafters
or brackets** visible under that overhang, a **fascia or barge board** closing the ends, a
**ridge** capping the two planes, and an **edge condition** at the gable and at the verge. Tiles,
shingles and thatch have thickness and a bottom edge that stands proud; a roof texture on a flat
plane has none of this and dies instantly under the clay test. Where water leaves the roof there
is a gutter, a spout, a drip, or a stone splash pad below.

**Foundations and ground contact.** Posts sit on pad stones. Buildings sit on plinths. Steps have
a nosing and a worn tread. Nothing in the real world meets the ground with a clean 90° edge and
no transition — there is a skirt, a plinth, a splash zone, packed earth, or a gutter.

**Storeys and stairs.** Balconies have balustrades with a handrail, newel posts terminating the
run, and something carrying the balcony out from the wall (brackets, cantilevered joists).

## Street furniture, lighting, signage

- **Lanterns / lamps:** a light chamber with an **opening you can reach into or through** to
  light, refill or clean; a shade or cap keeping rain off the flame; a shelf or bracket carrying
  the chamber; a post or wall bracket carrying that; a base plate or buried footing. A lantern
  with no way to light it is a sculpture of a lantern.
- **Signs:** a board with thickness, a frame or cleat holding it flat, brackets or chains
  hanging it, fixings into the wall, and a top edge that sheds water. Hanging signs swing on eyes.
- **Benches:** slats with gaps and thickness, a frame under them, legs with feet, fixings at every
  slat-to-frame junction, and a back attached to something.
- **Fences and railings:** posts (thicker, buried or set in a footing), rails spanning between
  them, infill between the rails, a cap rail, and joints where each rail meets each post.

## Props, tools, containers

- **Barrels:** staves with visible joints, iron hoops standing proud, a chime and croze at the
  ends, a bung hole, a lid if it opens.
- **Crates and boxes:** boards with thickness and gaps, corner battens, nail or screw heads, a
  lid with hinges or a lifting lip, rope handles or hand holes at the ends.
- **Baskets, sacks, bundles:** a weave or a slump that shows what is inside, a tie or a rim, a
  handle. Sacks fold and crease under their own weight where they touch.
- **Tools:** a working end, a haft, a ferrule or socket joining them, a grip worn where the hand
  goes, and a way to hang or store it.
- **Pots, buckets, vessels:** rim (rolled or thickened), a wall with real thickness visible at the
  rim, a base that is proud or footed, a handle attached at two points, a spout if it pours.

## Furniture

Legs meet the top through an apron, rail or bracket, not by intersecting it. Drawers have runners,
faces set into the carcass, and pulls. Doors have hinges and a catch. Shelves rest on cleats,
pegs, or dados. Anything with a soft top has a seam and a piping line where the fabric wraps.
Feet are separate from legs and worn on the underside.

## Vehicles, carts, boats

Wheels have hubs, spokes, a felloe and a tyre band; they sit on an axle that goes somewhere.
Something transfers load from body to axle (bolster, springs, leaf). There is a place to hitch or
push, a bed with sides that pin or drop, tie-down points and rope, a brake, and wear where the
load goes in and out. Boats have ribs inside the hull, a gunwale with thickness, thwarts, rowlocks
or a mast step, a rudder with pintles, and a bailing point.

## Terrain, paths, walls, water edges

- **Paths and paving:** individual stones or slabs with thickness and gaps, an edge restraint or
  kerb, worn centres and grown-in margins, and a level change with a step or ramp where it needs
  one. A path is not a texture on the ground plane.
- **Dry stone / retaining walls:** individual stones with beds and joints, through-stones, a
  batter (they lean back), a coping course on top, and a footing course wider than the wall.
- **Water edges:** a bank profile, a lip or coping where masonry meets water, steps or a landing
  where people reach the water, and a wet line.
- **Cliffs and rock:** bedding planes and jointing that run consistently through the mass, scree
  where material has fallen, and a soil/vegetation line. Noise-displaced blobs have none of this.

## Vegetation

Trees have root flare where trunk meets ground, branch collars where limbs leave the trunk, and
taper. Bark thickness shows at broken edges. Hedges have a cut face and a leggy base. Planted
things sit in something — a pot with a rim and drainage, a bed with an edge, a hole with disturbed
soil. Cards with a leaf texture fail the clay test by definition: turn textures off and a
billboard is a rectangle.

## Interiors and set dressing

Ceilings have joists or a cornice. Floors have boards with a direction, thickness at the
threshold, and a skirting where they meet walls. Anything mounted to a wall has a bracket and
fixings. Anything stored has a shelf or a hook. Cables and ropes hang in catenaries and terminate
somewhere real.

## The check that catches most misses

Ask: **"if this were built, what would fall off, leak, or be impossible to use?"**

- Nothing holds the roof up → add rafters and posts.
- Rain runs straight down the wall face → add the eave overhang and the drip.
- The lamp can't be lit → add the firebox opening.
- The lid can't be opened → add hinges and a lip.
- It would tip over → add the footing, the plinth, or the ballast.
- Nobody could carry it → add the handle, the rope eye, the grab rail.

Every one of those answers is geometry, and geometry is what survives the textures being turned
off.
